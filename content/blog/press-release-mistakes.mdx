We read everything before it goes out. Most articles need nothing. The ones we send back usually fail for the same handful of reasons, and none of them are about writing talent.

Names and details below are changed, but every case is one we have actually handled.

## 1. The headline was the company name

> *Acme Industries LLC Announces Important Company News*

That is not a headline, it is a filing. Nobody clicks it, no editor would run it, and search engines have nothing to work with. A headline should say what happened: *Acme cuts delivery times to under two hours across Texas*.

The fix takes one minute and roughly triples the chance anyone reads past it.

## 2. It promised a result

> *Our system guarantees a 300% increase in revenue.*

Newspapers do not run guarantees, and for good reason: the claim belongs to whoever makes it, but the page carrying it belongs to the paper. Say what the product does and what it has done for named customers. Drop the word "guarantee" entirely.

## 3. It was a landing page with paragraph breaks

Nine sentences, seven of them an instruction to buy. A press release is read by people who have not decided anything yet. The first paragraph has to be worth reading if you are never going to buy.

We usually ask for one thing: tell us what happened and why it matters to somebody who has never heard of you. The selling takes care of itself from there.

## 4. The news was six months old

Not automatically a problem, but if it reads as new and is not, somebody will notice, and the one who notices is often the customer you most wanted. Either date it honestly or find the part that is current.

## 5. There was nothing to link to

An article with no website, no product page and no profile behind it gives a reader nowhere to go and gives a search engine no reason to treat it as being about anything. Even a single page is enough. A social profile will do if that is genuinely all there is.

## 6. It was written for an industry nobody has explained

> *Our platform leverages distributed ledger primitives for cross-chain settlement finality.*

This may be exactly right and still unpublishable, because the reader in Cheyenne has no way in. One plain sentence early on — what it is, who uses it, what it replaces — makes the rest readable without making it less accurate.

## What we do instead of refusing

We send it back with the reason and what would fix it. The slot is held, nothing is lost, and the order waits. Most come back the same day and go out that afternoon.

The only things we will not publish at all are the ones no newspaper should carry: claims about health outcomes, financial promises, anything that needs a licence nobody has produced, and content about a named private person who has not agreed to be written about.
